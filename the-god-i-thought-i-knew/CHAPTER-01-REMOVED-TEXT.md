My mother nearly did not live to be my mother.
She was just a small girl. It was the cool of the evening, in the hills of Calabria, and her father was out working the family land. In the late afternoon her mother would gather up the children and go out to the little shack they had in the grove, to prepare dinner for the family over an open fire outside. Her father would see the sun dropping low toward the hills and know it was near time to finish for the day. When dinner was ready her mother would call across the grove, and they would come in and eat together as the light dimmed.
The children crouched in a ring around the flames, close to the warmth and the smell of the food. My mother, Rosina, was among them. One of her sisters, a child herself, not understanding what she was doing, took a burning log from the edge of the fire and slipped it under Rosina where she crouched.
Her dress caught.
It was her mother who saw it first. She did not stop to think. She snatched Rosina up off the ground and ran to an animals' drinking trough close by and plunged her daughter down into the water. Her mother's quickness saved her life. The fire had taken hold only where she had been crouching, and the water put it out before it could spread and do worse. But the burning she had already taken marked her, there, for the rest of her days. She was still a very small girl, and she carried that mark out of childhood and kept it her whole life.
One fire almost killed my mother when she was a little girl. But another fire was about to give her life. When the Holy Spirit came down, *they saw what seemed to be tongues of fire that separated and came to rest on each of them,* and that fire would one day catch hold of her and give her back far more than the first one ever took.
She did not know that yet. Years later, when she had grown into a young woman and the girls of her village were beginning to think about marrying, my mother turned instead toward God. The love she had for Him had always been in her, and it carried her all the way to the convent door, because the convent was the only place she knew where He was. I have also wondered if that scar played a part in her choosing the convent. Perhaps she could not bear the thought of standing before a man and uncovering that old shame. With God there was nothing to hide, for He had seen the fire and the scar and loved her anyway.
Either way, she wanted God, and she lived in a country that was certain it already had Him sorted.
It was Calabria, 1946. The toe of southern Italy, where the mountains come down hard to the sea and the villages cling to them like they are holding on for their lives. One of those villages, further inland, was called Fabrizia, and it was there, at eighteen, that Rosina entered the convent near her village, and settled in there, looking forward to a life poured out for God and for others, full of His love.
She would often tell me the stories of those two years, and there was real joy in some of them. She loved the habit and the headpiece. She shared a room with her best friend. In time they put her in charge of the grocery storeroom off the convent kitchen, her and one other nun, and it was there that one of her happiest memories was made, the day of the olives.
They had laid a whole crop of olives out on sheets of paper to dry in the sun, and when the olives were ready to be brought inside for seasoning, the two of them went out to carry them in. My mother took two corners of the paper. The other nun took the two corners on the far side. They lifted together, and the paper tore straight down the middle. Every olive they had dried went up into the air at once and came raining down across the floor. She was still laughing about it decades later, placing her hand over her mouth the way she did, the picture of the two of them standing there with a torn sheet of paper and a whole harvest rolling under the benches.
But there were other things she saw in that place, and those she did not laugh about.
The novices were taught that obedience came before understanding, that to lay down your own will was itself a way of giving yourself to God. The mother superior told them plainly that they would see and hear things they would not understand, and that it was not their place to understand them, only to obey. If they were told to plant the cauliflowers in the garden upside down, then upside down was how the cauliflowers went in, and not a word about it. I have no doubt the ones who taught this believed it, and meant it well, as a way of breaking the young of their pride. My mother understood the intention of it. She simply found that the more she was told not to ask, the more her own heart wanted to.
Then there was the matter of the meat. In the Catholic tradition of those years, no one was to eat meat on a Friday, out of reverence for the day the Lord was crucified. Yet week after week my mother was sent to carry the lunch and the dinner in to the priests on Fridays, and on the tray were meatballs, or some other meat. She did not understand it, and it was not her place to ask. There may well have been reasons she was never told, allowances made for the old or the unwell that a young novice was not meant to know about. I cannot say. She only knew that she was asked to keep a rule that others at the same table seemed free of, and that no one would explain to her why. She carried the tray. She kept her mouth shut. She kept her eyes open.
The thing that troubled her most was the Bible. In that convent, in those years, the novices were not permitted to own one. Bibles, she was told, were for the priests and the men of the cloth who had risen high enough to be trusted with them. When she asked what a person had to do to come into the Grace of God, the answer she was given was that it came only at the top of a long climb, rung by rung, year by year, through obedience and holiness, until one had at last been found worthy of it. I want to be fair to the ones who taught her this. They had come to God by that same road themselves, and they were handing on the only path they had ever been shown. But something in my mother could not rest under it. She did the arithmetic in her head, and her heart sank. *I might be an old woman before I reach the top of that ladder. And who is to say God will even take me after all of it?*
