# Chapter One
## In the Beginning

My mother came from a small village called Fabrizia, in Calabria, Italy. Even as a child she had a heart after God, and when she turned eighteen she decided to join the local convent.

What troubled her most was the Bible. In that convent, in those years, the novices were not permitted to own one. Bibles, she was told, were for the priests and the men of the cloth who had risen high enough to be trusted with them. When she asked what a person had to do to come into the Grace of God, the answer she was given was that it came only at the top of a long climb, rung by rung, year by year, through obedience and holiness, until one had at last been found worthy of it. I want to be fair to the ones who taught her this. They had come to God by that same road themselves, and they were handing on the only path they had ever been shown. But something in my mother could not rest under it. She did the arithmetic in her head, and her heart sank. *I might be an old woman before I reach the top of that ladder. And who is to say God will even take me after all of it?*

She had come to the convent to find God for herself, to know Him the way you know someone you love. She was beginning to fear that in this place He would always be Someone she was told about by others, and never Someone she was allowed to meet on her own.

Then a man died, and the door opened.

One of the older priests passed away, and a maintenance man was given the job of emptying the dead man's room and burning everything in it. He had built a pile of clothes and belongings near the doorway, ready to take out and set alight. My mother happened to be walking past that morning, and something in the pile caught her eye. A thick black book.

She knew exactly what it was. She asked the man, as evenly as she could manage, whether she might have it. He shrugged. He was about to burn the lot, so what did it matter to him. Take it, he said. She slid the Bible inside her cloak, thanked him, and kept walking, her heart slamming against her ribs the whole length of the corridor. A novice was not allowed to so much as touch one of these. She had just hidden one against her own body.

From that day she read it every moment she could steal, and what she was looking for was simple. She wanted to know what a person had to do to be in the Grace of God. But the stolen minutes were few, and the guilt of hiding the book sat heavy on her, and the more she read, the more she felt a gap open between the words on the page and the life she was living inside those walls. After two years she finally let herself ask the question she had been circling. Was she any closer to God than the day she had walked in? She was not. If anything she felt further away, not because the people around her were unkind, for they were not, but because the thing she had come for, God Himself, near enough to touch, seemed always to be kept one more rung up a ladder she could not climb.

So one day she went into the chapel, knelt down in front of the crucifix, and prayed the most honest prayer of her young life.

"God, I came here to be close to You. To know You for myself. But here I am not even allowed a Bible of my own, and I am told that only those who have climbed high enough into Your Grace are fit to read it and understand it. What must I do to come into Your Grace? If You are not to be found in this place, and I am not meant to be here, then please, take me out of it."

She finished in tears, and left it with God.

That very week, He answered.

A pain came up in the lower part of her stomach so fierce she could barely stand. The infirmary took one look and sent her straight to hospital, where they found an uncommonly inflamed appendix and operated on her at once. In those days the rule was firm. Any nun sent to hospital had to recover at home, under the care of her own family, until she was well enough to return. So, with her stitches still fresh, my mother was sent back down the mountain to her parents' house. She did not yet understand that the appendix bursting in her side was the first piece of a puzzle God had already begun to lay out across her whole life.

Her parents, Salvatore and Maria Rosaria, owned a small home just outside the village, with their fields around it, which they worked themselves and sometimes hired men to help with. They grew olives among other things, and pressed them, and sold the oil from the house, so that people from the village and travellers passing along that road would stop in to buy from them. My mother spent her recovery in an upstairs room, and from its window she could see across to where a team of young men were labouring in the fields. She noticed something strange about them. Every so often, for no reason she could see, one or other of them would stop, lift his hands into the air, shout out a few words she could not make out, and then bend straight back to the work. It happened again and again. It got under her skin. She could not figure out what they were shouting.

So she asked her father.

Yes, he had noticed them. In fact, he said, there was a story behind those boys. Earlier that month a couple had come through the village on a horse and cart, on their way to a town further on, and they had needed somewhere to spend the night. Her father had taken them in. By morning they had become friends. While they were under his roof the couple had seen how badly the family needed labour for the fields, and to thank him for his hospitality they had promised to send their eldest son and a few of his friends for a week to help bring the harvest in.

That was who the young men were. As for the lifting of hands and the shouting, well, her father said, it had something to do with their religion. While they were staying, the couple had tried to share their faith with the family, and the strange thing was that everything they said could be opened up and shown straight from the Bible they carried with them. One thing in particular her father could not get out of his head. They had shown him a verse, and made him read it with his own eyes, from the book of Leviticus.

*Do not make idols or set up an image or a sacred stone for yourselves, and do not place a carved stone in your land to bow down before it. I am the Lord your God.* Leviticus, chapter twenty-six, verse one.

"After I read it for myself," her father admitted, "I did not know what to say to them. Our own religion has us doing the very thing this Book forbids. No wonder we are not encouraged to read it. I was almost ashamed." He thought the couple were called Evangelists. He told his daughter that he and her mother were taking what they had heard very seriously, because these were people who lived clean and godly lives, who were generous to a fault, and whose every word and action seemed to revolve around God and the Bible.

That was all my mother needed to hear. She asked her father if he would invite the young man in for lunch. He agreed, and went to fetch him.

The young man came in, introduced himself, and thanked them for having him. His name was Vittorio. They settled around the table, and before they had so much as picked up a fork my mother asked the question that had been burning in her since she first saw the men in the field. What was all that, the lifting of the hands, the shouting?

Vittorio told her they were praise breaks. Moments he took, right in the middle of the work, to give the glory back to God for the Grace that had been given to him, freely, for his salvation and the forgiveness of his sins. Then he began to tell her, plainly and without any ceremony, of the things he had seen God do, and the encounters he had had with Him.

My mother nearly fell off her chair.

*I have been in a convent for over two years searching for God,* she thought, *and here is a labourer at my father's table speaking about God as though He were his own Father. Telling me He is near. Telling me He gives His Grace to anyone who comes to Him by faith in His Son, Jesus Christ.* The ladder she had been told to climb for the rest of her life, and here was a young man already standing at the top of it, talking like he lived there. *That is the God I have been looking for.*

Then Vittorio paused before the food, and looked at her habit.

"I can see you are a nun," he said. "I take it you are a woman of prayer. Are you going to give thanks to God for the food?"

She faltered. "I have not brought my prayer book. I left it at the convent."

"Well then," he said, "may I pray? I do not need a prayer book."

Again she was stunned. She looked to her father, who nodded for her to let him. Vittorio rose to his feet, lifted his hands toward heaven, and prayed.

"God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, we come before You today to lift Your name above every name, together with these dear children of Yours. My prayer is that You would reveal Yourself to them as You revealed Yourself to me." He said a few more things she would never afterward be able to recall, and closed by thanking God for the food, and sat down.

By the time he said amen, my mother was undone. The fire had found her at last, the other fire, and this time it did not burn her. It warmed her, all the way through, and it left no scar. She never could remember a single thing she ate at that table. What she remembered was that she had found the God she had been searching for, and she had found Him in the prayer of a poor, unschooled shepherd boy. In that moment she understood that getting sick, and being sent home, had not been bad luck at all. It had been the first piece of a divine plan, the opening move in a puzzle God was quietly assembling so that she would come to know Him.

By the end of that lunch the whole family had given their lives to Jesus. They repented of their sins and were born again, just as the Bible says: *Very truly I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God unless they are born again.* John, chapter three, verse three.

But my mother was not finished with Vittorio. She kept him there the whole afternoon, soaking up everything he knew, question after question, until at last he told her his dream. One day he wanted to become a pastor. He wanted to build a church for God.

Something turned over inside her. *This is where I belong. With a man who loves God enough to want to build Him a house.* She never went back to the convent. She had walked out of that place with nothing but a hidden Bible and a broken heart, and within the space of a single afternoon God had handed her two things at once. The God she had crossed her whole young life to find, and the next piece of the puzzle. The young man at her father's table. Vittorio, the man she would marry for the love they both carried for God.

Vittorio was the man who would one day become my father.

He went home that week and made sure of something. His own father was a pastor, and Vittorio had him send an elder down to my mother's parents' house to hold meetings every week. That is how the first evangelical church in that town was born, in the front room of my grandparents' house. The elder they sent was a man named Maiolo, and they say he was meek and gentle as a dove. As soon as the family had been baptised in water and in the Holy Spirit, the little group did not just grow. It caught fire.

My mother caught fire with it. She told me that when the Holy Spirit fell on her, the shyness she had carried her whole life simply burned away. The timidity, the fear of what people thought of her, gone. In its place came a boldness she had never known. The girl who had crept down a convent corridor with a Bible hidden in her cloak now stood up in her own village and preached, and many believed.

Word got around fast, because in those meetings things were happening that people could not explain. The sick were healed. Men and women were baptised in the Holy Spirit and spoke in tongues, exactly as the Bible had promised: *And these signs will accompany those who believe: in my name they will drive out demons; they will speak in new tongues.* Mark, chapter sixteen, verse seventeen. The little nun who had walked away from the convent was now causing the priests of her village a great deal of trouble.

When the convent finally understood that she was not coming back, that she had, in their words, changed religion, the priests were furious. To lose a nun to another faith was a public shame for the Catholic Church. So they began sending nuns my mother had befriended inside the convent down to her parents' house, one after another, to talk her into returning and to put a stop to what she was doing.

It did not go the way they planned. Each time a nun came to win my mother back, my mother used the visit to preach to her instead. She told them what she had found. That the Grace of God was not a prize reserved for priests and vicars at the top of some ladder, but a free gift for every single person who would believe in Jesus. That the only way to the Father was through His Son, not through the Church and not through any man, just as the Bible says in John, chapter fourteen, verse six. She had found the one true road to heaven, and it was paved with nothing but the unconditional love of God and His free gift of salvation. When the nuns carried those words back to the convent, the priests realised they were losing the battle. Worse, they were losing nuns. They stopped sending them, for fear of how many more they might lose to those simple biblical truths.

So they tried something harder.

This was just after the Second World War, and in those days it was still unlawful to hold unregistered gatherings, public or private. The priests knew it. They began sending children to throw rocks at my mother's windows during the prayer meetings, to break them up, knowing that since the meetings themselves were illegal there was nothing she could do in reply. But the rocks did not stop the meetings, and they did not scatter the people. So the priests went one step further. They took my mother to court, piling up every accusation they could think of, the illegal gatherings chief among them, hoping a judge would shut the whole thing down and choke the movement before it could spread.

Word of the court case ran through the town like a fever. It had the feel of David going up against Goliath, one young woman standing alone against the might of the Church, and half of Fabrizia turned up to see it. On the day, the room was packed wall to wall, and the air in it was tight with expectation.

The priest opened with both barrels. He stood and threw every charge he had at my mother. What an unfaithful, disgraceful woman she was, a traitor to the Catholic Church. How she was dragging the whole town off into some new religion. How she was performing miraculous healings without the Church's permission, holding meetings without a permit, on and on it went.

When it was her turn, my mother did not deny the one thing she was guilty of. She told the judge plainly that no, she did not have a permit for the meetings in her house. But she wanted him to understand what those meetings actually were. They were not political. They taught people to pray, and to respect the government they had elected. Most of all, they brought eternal hope to people's souls and healing to their bodies.

Then she told the judge one more thing. That the priests had been sending children to smash her windows while she and her people were at prayer. She knew she had been in the wrong not to seek permission, she said. But that did not give the priest the right to break her windows.

The judge turned to the priest. Was it true?

The priest knew some of those very children were sitting in the room. To deny it was to be caught in a lie in front of the whole town. He admitted it.

The judge rose to his feet.

"You brood of vipers," he said. "Your souls are as black on the inside as your garments are black on the outside." He turned to my mother. He found that this young woman had done no wrong to the community whatsoever. He did not stop there. He granted her the authority to hold gatherings in her house freely. As compensation for what had been done to her, he ruled that she could also hold meetings in the town square itself, as often as she liked and for as long as she liked. "And if anyone gives her any trouble," he said, "they will answer to me."

The hammer came down on the desk. Case closed.

You can imagine the roar that went up in that courtroom. The crowd erupted, cheering and shouting for the sheer joy of it, and the whole place was charged with a feeling of freedom, because something had just broken open. A door had swung wide for the Gospel to be preached out in the open in that corner of Italy. From then on there were not only meetings in my grandparents' house. There were open-air meetings in the town square, and many more came to the Lord.

That is where it began. A girl who wanted God, a hidden Bible, a shepherd boy's prayer, and a judge who threw the doors of a whole town open. None of them knew yet what God was building. They only knew He had started.

There is a footnote to my mother's story, and it did not reach me until forty years later, on the other side of the world.

One day in 1990 I met an Italian pastor in Sydney by the name of Charlie Fazio. I asked him where in Italy he was from, and he told me he came from a village in Calabria called Fabrizia. I said my mother was born and raised in that very village. He asked her name. I told him. Rosina Iacopetta.

"No," he said. "No way." He asked me a few more questions, turning it over, and then he was sure of it. "I know your mother," he said. Then he asked me something that made the hair stand up on my arms. "Did she ever tell you a story, from when she was a young woman in Fabrizia, about being persecuted by the priests? About some boys who used to come and smash her windows while they were at prayer?"

Yes, I told him. She had.

"My brother and I," he said, "were two of the rascals the priests sent to smash your mother's windows."

I could not take it in. The boys who had thrown stones at my mother's prayer meetings were now pastors of the very Gospel she had been on her knees praying for. I said to him, why don't we ring her right now and let her know what you have become. So we did. When my mother heard the news, she was so delighted she invited Charlie and his wife to come up and stay a few days with her on the Gold Coast, where she was by then living. They came, and they sat and reminisced, and as they pieced it all together we discovered that the Fazio family had been saved in the very church my grandfather had pioneered in those days.

The stones had been thrown. The boys who threw them grew up to preach. That is the kind of God my mother found at her father's table, the God I am going to spend this whole book trying to tell you about. The God I thought I knew.
