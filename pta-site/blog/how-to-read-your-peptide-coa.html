How to read your peptide COA like a chemist

By the PTA Lab Team·6 min read·Updated July 2026

A Certificate of Analysis (COA) is the document that turns "trust me" into "here's the proof". But only if you can read it — and only if it's real. Here's how to interpret every part of yours, and how to spot the fakes doing the rounds.

The five things that actually matter

1. Purity (% area)

The headline number. In HPLC, purity is the area of the target peak divided by the area of all detected peaks, as a percentage. Higher is better. For most research peptides, a genuine, well-made sample sits comfortably in the high 90s. Anything materially below the stated specification is a red flag.

2. Identity (mass spectrometry)

Purity tells you how much of the target is present; identity confirms it's the right target at all. Mass spec measures molecular weight against the expected value. A pure vial of the wrong compound is still the wrong compound — identity is what catches substitution.

3. The chromatogram

The squiggly trace. One tall, clean peak is what you want. A forest of smaller peaks alongside it means impurities and byproducts. The certificate should show the trace, not just quote a number — the picture is much harder to fake than a figure typed into a template.

A number can be typed into any document. A clean chromatogram, a matching mass, and a verifiable reference are much harder to fabricate.

4. Batch & sample identifiers

A real COA ties to a specific sample and batch. If a seller hands you a COA, check the batch matches the vial you were sent. A certificate for a different batch is worthless for the vial in your hand — this is the single most common trick.

5. Who issued it, and can you verify it

Look for the issuing laboratory and a way to confirm authenticity — a reference number, and ideally a scan-to-verify code. A COA that can't be independently checked is just a nice PDF.

Biggest red flag of all: a COA supplied by the seller, for a batch you can't match to your vial, from a lab you can't verify. If you didn't commission the test yourself, treat it as marketing, not proof.

Why buyer-supplied COAs fall short

Even genuine seller COAs share one weakness: they test a sample the seller chose, at a time the seller chose. The safest signal is a test you commissioned, on the exact vial you were sent. That's the whole point of independent testing — the lab has no stake in the answer.

What a PTA COA gives you

  • HPLC purity to the decimal, with the chromatogram shown.
  • Mass-spec identity confirmation against the expected molecular weight.
  • A clear pass/fail against specification.
  • A unique reference and scan-to-verify code, issued via a TGA-approved partner lab.

Learn to read it once, and you'll never look at a seller's screenshot the same way again.
